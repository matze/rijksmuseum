## timeline
Five and a half metres high and eight metres wide, the largest painting in the museum. The
Duke of Wellington sits at the centre on his horse Copenhagen, at the moment the news
reaches him that Prussian help is on its way. Lying wounded on a stretcher at the lower
left is the Dutch Prince of Orange, later King William II — which is the reason a Dutch
museum owns the picture at all.

## closer
The canvas has two halves. Everything around Wellington is already victory; everything to
the left and right of him is still costing somebody a limb.

## detail
It is a group portrait as much as a battle. Pieneman spent nearly four months in London in
1821, working from a studio Wellington let him set up in Apsley House; the duke sat for
him, arranged sittings with other commanders, and had Copenhagen brought over to be
painted from life. The finished canvas took almost three years.

Wellington may have meant to buy it. Instead the Prince of Orange did, for 40,000
guilders, a sum his father William I actually paid. Pieneman made it a condition of the
sale that he could show the painting in London first: a pavilion was built for it in Hyde
Park, and it drew crowds there for some ten months from May 1825, including the tenth
anniversary of the battle.

## look
1. Wellington at the centre, at the moment the message reaches him.
2. The wounded Prince of Orange on his stretcher, low and to the left.
3. The captured French imperial eagles being waved aloft by British dragoons.
