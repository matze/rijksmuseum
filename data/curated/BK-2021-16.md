## timeline
Fifty-six centimetres of boxwood, carved around 1400 by Claus Sluter of Haarlem, court
sculptor to the Duke of Burgundy — the man who took medieval sculpture from pattern to
observation. The cross is not a cross. It is a sawn-off tree, roots and all.

## closer
Go round to the base and find the lioness. She is the reason the tree is a tree.

## detail
Sluter is one of the very few sculptors of the period whose name survives attached to
work, and he is credited with introducing an unprecedented realism into European
sculpture around 1400. The evidence is in the drapery here: Mary's cloak and John's
robe hang and fold as cloth actually hangs, which is not how carving had been done.

The cross is carved as a truncated living trunk, rooted in a rocky ground, with a dead
sawn branch halfway up where an angel hovers. Underneath the roots is a hollow, and in it
a lioness tends her cubs — an old symbol of the resurrection, tucked where you have to
crouch to find it.
