## timeline
A man lays his hand on a woman's breast and she lays hers on top of it, and that is the
whole event. Nobody knows for certain who they are. The picture survives the not
knowing, because what it is actually about is two hands agreeing.

## detail
The title is a nineteenth-century mistake that stuck. An Amsterdam collector decided the
scene showed a Jewish father fastening a necklace on his daughter's wedding day; nobody
accepts that now, and no replacement has won. The museum reads the pair as Isaac and
Rebecca, who told King Abimelech they were brother and sister so that Isaac would not be
killed for her, and were given away by exactly this kind of unguarded moment. Rembrandt
had drawn that subject some years earlier. Others have proposed his son Titus, or the
poet Miguel de Barrios, or Abraham and Sarah, or Boaz and Ruth.

What is not in doubt is the handling. The gold of the man's sleeve is laid on in ridges
thick enough to catch the gallery light and then cut into while still wet, and her red
skirt is worked the same way. Rembrandt's biographer Christopher White called the result
one of the greatest expressions in painting of spiritual and physical love fused
together, which is a large claim that the picture does not obviously fail.
