## timeline
You are in the dark, looking through an open doorway into a lit room, past a curtain that
has been pulled aside. A maid has just handed her mistress a letter; the woman has a
cittern in her lap and has looked up from it. Everything about the framing says that you
are not supposed to be seeing this.

## closer
Look at the two pictures on the back wall. The lower one is a stormy sea — and it is the
only seascape anywhere in Vermeer's work.

## detail
The props carry the argument. A cittern in Dutch painting of the period stands for love,
often the carnal sort; the slipper kicked off in the foreground says much the same thing;
the broom and the chair left in the doorway say that the housework has been set down
mid-task. Above the seascape hangs a landscape with a traveller on a sandy road, which is
usually read as the absent man who wrote the letter.

In 1971 the painting was stolen from a loan exhibition in Brussels. The thief hid in a
cupboard until closing time, found the frame would not fit through the window, cut the
canvas out with a potato peeler and eventually kept it in his pillowcase. His demand was
200 million Belgian francs for famine relief in East Pakistan. It came back eight days
later, badly damaged, and took almost a year to restore.
