## timeline
A kitchen maid pours milk into an earthenware bowl and nothing else in the room moves.
Vermeer took the plainest job in the house and painted it at the scale and seriousness
of an altarpiece: she stands like a statue, and the only thing falling is the milk.

## closer
Everything is still except the stream. Find the moment the painting decides to hold, and
you have found what Vermeer is for.

## detail
She would have been called a kitchen maid rather than a milkmaid — women who milked cows
were a different job — and a maid-of-all-work is not what a seventeenth-century painter
gave a wall this size to. The canvas is 45.5 by 41 centimetres, which is small; the
figure inside it is not.

The museum's own note points at the light: hundreds of coloured dots laid over the
surface of things, so that the bread crusts stay rough and the maid's forearms stay
heavy. Half her face is in shadow, and neither her eyes nor her mouth will say whether
she is concentrating or thinking about something else entirely.

Behind her, low on the wall, runs a row of Delft tiles — Cupid on the left of them, a
figure with a pole on the right — and a wooden foot warmer sits on the floor. Kitchen
maids carried a reputation in Dutch painting for being inclined to love, and those two
objects are how the picture mentions it without saying it.
