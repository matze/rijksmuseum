## timeline
Five cloth inspectors and their servant look up from the books, as though somebody had
just opened the door — which is to say, as though you had. Rembrandt painted it in 1662,
long after the Amsterdam élite had stopped queueing at his studio, and it is the last
great group portrait he made.

## detail
The sitters were the wardens of the Amsterdam drapers' guild, whose job was to test
cloth against sample pieces — staal in Dutch, which is where their name comes from.
Quality ran to four grades, marked by pressing one to four seals into small lead slugs
attached to the bolt. The post was unpaid and honorary, the term ran a year from Good
Friday, and this board met three times a week. They were, between them, two Catholics, a
Mennonite, a Remonstrant and a Reformed Protestant. From the left: Jacob van Loon,
Volckert Jansz, Willem van Doeyenburg, the servant Frans Hendricksz Bel standing behind,
Aernout van der Mye and Jochem de Neve.

By the 1650s Rembrandt's finances had collapsed and he was renting a house on the
Rozengracht. The commission mattered, and the painting hung in the guild's own hall, the
Staalhof, until 1771. For a long time the man on the right was thought to be holding a
bag of stamps; the 1991 restoration established that it is a pair of gloves.
